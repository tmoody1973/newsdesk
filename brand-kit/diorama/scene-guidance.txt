SCENE authoring rules — the paper-diorama kit

Source: ~/.claude/skills/vox-motion-graphics/references/diorama-doc.md, the
"WHO BLINKS?" playbook, reverse-engineered from a reference video and then
battle-tested on a nuclear-treaty explainer. Everything below that reads like an
opinion was paid for in failed renders. Read the source before changing any of
it; this file is where the parts that survived the translation to Newsdesk live,
and it says plainly which parts did not survive and why.

Use this kit when the brief says cinematic, dramatic, or investigative, or when
the topic is geopolitics, money, or power. The house kit is an editorial collage
and it explains; this one is a documentary diorama and it accuses.


WHAT THE WORLD IS

A miniature three-dimensional world built entirely out of aged sepia newspaper
and cardboard, shot like a documentary: warm tungsten light, deep shadows, macro
tilt-shift, film grain, dust in the air. Figures are monochrome archival cutouts
with black censor bars over their eyes. Handcrafted paper materials only —
nothing photoreal, nothing live-action, no 3D-render look.

Exactly one burnt-orange element per scene. In a monochrome world the single
accent is not decoration, it is the sentence: it says WHERE TO LOOK. Two orange
elements in one frame is not a richer image, it is two sentences spoken at once,
and it is the fastest way to make this style look cheap. The through-line object
IS the orange element (through-lines.yaml) — so if the scene already has an
orange prop and the through-line arrives orange as well, the block is wrong.

Measured on the first live run (diorama-smoke, 2026-08-02): five of six blocks
came back on-style; block 2's note-stack rendered WALNUT BROWN with no orange
anywhere in frame. One miss in six, from a prompt whose SCENE names burnt orange
four times and closes on "the only colour anywhere in the frame". Read it as
stochastic rather than as a prompt defect — but read every frame, because a
scene that loses the accent has lost the through-line, and it does not announce
itself in any log. Re-roll that block; do not re-write the prompt around it.


THE FAKE-ONER — the shape every block is written in

Every clip is ONE continuous, high-energy FPV camera move with aggressive speed
ramps, and every clip begins and ends in full motion blur. That is what makes six
hard cuts read as a single unbroken shot instead of six pretty postcards. A
sequence of disconnected scenes reads as a museum slideshow; this is the whole
difference between a banger and a slideshow, and it costs nothing extra to
render.

The shape, in the source's own order:

  * The shot EMERGES from the motion-blurred element the previous shot ended in
    — the dust, the whip, the fall, the flare. Block 1 emerges from black,
    already moving.
  * One IMPACT MOMENT roughly every three seconds: a slam, a stamp, a shockwave,
    a snap, a rip. Ten seconds of drift with one event at the end is the failure
    mode; three events is the target.
  * At least one speed ramp per block, and alternate extreme macro against wide
    between blocks. Whiplash the scale. Never two identically framed blocks
    running.
  * The shot ENDS fully motion-blurred mid-dive, mid-whip, mid-fall or mid-flare.
    It does not settle. The one exception is the final block, which settles for
    the first and only time and holds — that landing is only worth anything
    because nothing before it landed.
  * SOUND DESIGN: three to five concrete diegetic events, named as events rather
    than as a mood. "Guillotine slam with dust whump, three accelerating page
    rips, one massive stamp punch, rushing paper wind" is the register. Always
    close with "No speech." — the narration is added in assembly and a clip that
    invents its own voice is unusable.

The worked example in the source is worth reading in full before writing the
first block of any story. Its opening block does four things in ten seconds — a
censor bar guillotining down onto a printed eye, three accelerating page rips
through successive portraits, a stamp punching a word across a treaty, and a
dive through the torn fibres into paper dust — and every one of them is a
concrete physical event rather than an adjective.


THE LETTERPRESS LABEL — this kit's signature, and its one narrowing

Unlike the house style, which bans all in-clip text, this style CARRIES short
letterpress text on props. That is its signature and it is the only reason the
platform exclusion constant was ever split (policy.yaml POL-2 v3, POL-4).

The rules, all of them enforced somewhere other than here:

  * ONE label per scene. Not one per prop, not one per surface.
  * Four words maximum — POL-4's element budget, enforced in claims.py. The
    source says one or two words, or a number, is the sweet spot: EXPIRED, 1,000,
    AUGUST. Both facts are true and they are not in tension: four is the ceiling
    at which models start duplicating or garbling, and two is where the frame
    actually looks composed. Write toward two.
  * It must map to a fact the journalist entered. claims.py validates the label
    exactly as it validates a spoken claim — a label that traces to nothing is
    refused and repaired at $0, before a picture is bought. A word printed on a
    prop is a claim in three words, and it borrows the credibility of the
    sentence spoken over it.
  * Always described as distressed letterpress on a TORN BURNT-ORANGE PAPER
    ELEMENT — a stamp, a band, a tag. The label rides the accent; it never floats.
  * Always fenced in the negative for that block: no text anywhere except the
    label itself, no gibberish letters, no captions, no watermark.

WIRED (Task 11b): `ScriptBlock.label` is emitted by the script stage,
validated by claims.py, survives the state round trip, and now reaches the
prompt. `BlockPrompt.build(..., label=...)` splices the sentence above into
SCENE and appends the fence to NEGATIVE, only when a label is present — a
label-less block, and every house block, renders byte-identical to before this
was wired. `gate.check(prompt, ..., labels=(block.label,))` is what sources it:
POL-4 treats a quoted SCENE string as legitimate when it matches an entered
fact ID (as before) OR case-insensitively equals one of the attested labels.
`stage_gate` and `stage_blocks` in pipeline.py both build every block's prompt
through one shared method (`Pipeline._block_prompts`) precisely so the gate
checks the same bytes the render stage pays for — see policy.yaml POL-4's v4
changelog entry for the full account of what the sourcing test used to say.

WHAT THE FIRST LIVE RENDER SHOWED (diorama-smoke block 3, 2026-08-02). The
label WATERTOWN rendered correctly spelled — and DUPLICATED onto two separate
props in the same frame. "doubled text" is in this kit's negative.txt and did
not prevent it, which is the negation mechanism working exactly as the house
guidance says it does: a negative acts only after the positive prompt has
already rendered the thing, and cancels it against whatever occupies that
region. Nothing here occupies "the second prop", so there is nothing to cancel
against. The lever is positive and it is the fence's own wording: say WHERE the
one label is, on WHICH element, and that everything else in frame is unprinted.
One label, one surface, named — the same rule the surface clauses in
through-lines.yaml apply to typography-carrying objects, applied to the label
itself. Until that is written into the spliced sentence, expect roughly one
duplication per six blocks and check every frame for it.


THE MODERATION MAP — hard-won, and the reason this file exists

These are engine refusals, not policy. Our own gate is upstream of all of it.

  * NAMED POLITICIANS IN A VIDEO PROMPT FAIL. The job submits fine and dies at
    render, which is the worst shape a failure can have — you find out after the
    wait. Names are fine in the TTS narration; keep them out of the picture.
  * CLOSE-UP RECOGNISABLE STATESMAN FACES fail even when unnamed and only
    described. The reference route around this was to send face-forward blocks to
    a different engine; here, don't write them. Stay at mid-shot or wider.
  * MID-SHOT AND FULL-BODY descriptions of unnamed leaders pass — "a heavyset
    elderly statesman with a long red tie", "a compact stern figure in a dark
    coat". That is the register to write in.
  * CENSOR BARS OVER THE EYES do two jobs at once: they are the style signature,
    and they defuse the likeness question before it is asked. Use them on every
    figure, not only the ones that worry you.
  * "MUSHROOM CLOUD" TRIPS AN NSFW FLAG. The replacement on the reference story
    was an hourglass silhouette, which fit the deadline theme better than the
    thing it replaced — see the hourglass entry in through-lines.yaml. When a
    literal image of a catastrophe is refused, reach for the silhouette that
    carries the same meaning rather than a synonym for the same picture.


WHAT DID NOT TRANSFER FROM THE SOURCE

The source is written for a different provider stack. These are Newsdesk
decisions, already made; they are recorded here so nobody re-derives them from
the reference and quietly changes the pipeline back.

  * ASPECT: 9:16, not the reference's 16:9. Newsdesk delivers 9:16 end to end,
    the subtitle PlayRes is 1080x1920, and assembly is not aspect-aware. DO NOT
    "FIX" THIS BACK to match the reference. This note is here, in guidance,
    rather than in style-tokens.txt, because that file is sent to the provider
    verbatim — an explanation of ourselves would have shipped inside the prompt
    as an instruction about aspect ratio.
  * ENGINE: seedance-1-0-pro-fast-251015. The source specifies seedance_2_0 at
    reference grade, and 2.0 is genuinely better-looking; it bills per second,
    which is about $5.40 for six ten-second blocks against $0.13. That is 41x the
    video cost of a run. Escalate only if the look demonstrably fails on 1.0,
    and say so with a frame rather than an impression.
  * STYLE CONSISTENCY: each block's own still, passed through `first_frame` —
    never image references. The source keeps a reusable style-key job id and a
    library of prop assets and attaches them as `image_references`. On this stack
    that made consistency WORSE: two scenes generated from one key came back with
    a blue ground and a tan ground, while naming the palette in text locked it.
    bria-fibo accepts `reference_images`, `image` and `image_url`, returns 200 on
    all three, and reads none of them. GMI accepting a parameter has never meant
    GMI reading it. The text lock is what works here.
  * PRESET NOTICES and `declined_preset_id`: a provider-specific handshake on the
    reference stack. Nothing equivalent exists on ours.
  * MUSIC: out of scope for this kit. The engine's native drone and SFX bed
    survives assembly under the voiceover, and the AUDIO field is what shapes it.


TEXT BLEED — the house rules still apply, and they are not optional here

Everything in brand-kit/scene-guidance.txt about keeping unwanted text out of a
frame holds in this world too, and matters MORE, because this world is built out
of newspaper. The short version, with the house file as the full account:

  1. Never append a negation list to the end of SCENE. Keyword negation is what
     the model handles worst, and it must render a thing before it can cancel it.
  2. Name blankness as a positive property of the object, in the SAME SENTENCE
     that introduces the object. "An unprinted aged paper document, an unbroken
     blank field" works; "a document (no text)" does not.
  3. Say what IS on the surface. Absence alone is not a subject.
  4. "as though the type were never set" is a useful closer — it frames the
     blankness as the object's history rather than as a restriction.
  5. Objects that natively carry typography — documents, banknotes, front pages,
     signs — need rules 2 and 3 applied explicitly. In through-lines.yaml those
     entries are marked `lettering_risk: high` and carry a `surface` clause.
  6. None of this is sufficient. POL-6's post-render vision check is the
     enforcement; these rules only reduce how often it has to fire.

Avoid the phrase "the words" anywhere in a framing clause. POL-4's deterministic
half reads it as a request for on-screen text and blocks the block — caught by
tests/test_kits.py running the gate against this kit's own menu.


KNOWN GAP, so the first render is read correctly

scene.py's six-beat vocabulary — the shot scales, motifs, impacts, entries,
exits and sound events — is still written in HOUSE language, and some of it names
house materials ("a big empty field of cream card", "a hand-drawn marker circle
SNAPS closed"). The ground, the accent and the object come from this kit; those
six arrays do not, yet. Expect a little collage vocabulary in the first renders,
and if it reads as house-style contamination rather than as texture, that is the
next thing to make kit-aware — not the style tokens, which are already right.
